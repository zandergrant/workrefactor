Common starting points

The problems behind the busywork.

Automation is useful when it removes a real constraint. Often the first improvement is changing the process before writing a line of code.

01

Manual reporting

People spend hours collecting updates from several tools, rebuilding spreadsheets and checking which version is current.

02

Requests without clear ownership

Work enters through email, forms and chat, but responsibility, status and next steps are difficult to see.

03

Handoffs between teams

Approvals stall and information gets copied because the next team doesn't have what it needs to proceed.

04

Software that doesn't quite fit

Existing systems hold the data, but the people doing the work need a smaller, clearer way to review it and take action.

Our approach

Start with the process, not a tool purchase.

We work from actual examples of the job. The scope and technology follow what the team needs, rather than a predetermined software stack.

01

Understand the work as it happens

Follow real requests, records and handoffs from start to finish. Identify duplicate entry, unclear decisions, waiting time and exceptions. Establish a baseline for the problem you're trying to change.

Useful output: A current-state map and a short list of opportunities, ordered by impact and feasibility.

02

Simplify the operating rules

Decide who owns each step, what information is required and what should happen when the ordinary path fails. Remove unnecessary steps before automating them.

Useful output: A simpler proposed workflow with clear responsibilities and acceptance checks.

03

Connect or build the smallest useful solution

Start with configuration and integrations where they work. When they don't, build a focused dashboard, request interface or internal app around the existing source of data. Keep meaningful decisions with the responsible people.

Useful output: A working pilot your team can actually test, with ownership and failure cases documented.

04

Measure, improve and hand over

Compare the new process against the baseline. Check time spent, quality, exceptions and ongoing maintenance, then refine what isn't working. Leave the team with a practical way to operate and update the system.

Useful output: An evidence-based decision to keep, adjust or stop the change.

Engagements

What the work can include.

Not every project needs all of these. The point is to improve the work, not to sell the most complicated implementation.

Workflow and systems audit

Trace recurring work, understand its cost, identify constraints and agree on a practical sequence of improvements.

Process redesign

Simplify intake, approvals, handoffs and exception handling so the next person can act without another status meeting.

Integration and automation

Make useful information move between existing tools, with safeguards for validation, duplicates, failures and changes.

Internal applications and dashboards

Build small operational interfaces or reporting tools when a spreadsheet or existing platform no longer supports the work well.

AI inside a workflow

Test narrow jobs such as classification or extraction, with defined inputs, human review and measurable quality checks.

Ongoing improvement

Review results after implementation and maintain or adjust the processes and tools as the business changes.

Questions before starting

A few things worth knowing.

Do we need to replace our software?

Usually not. The first question is whether existing tools can be configured or connected to remove the friction. A new system creates maintenance work, so it needs to earn its place.

What size projects do you take on?

The scope starts with the problem, not a fixed package. A focused workflow audit or pilot can be more useful than a large implementation. We evaluate fit after understanding the work and the decision-makers involved.

Can we start with a spreadsheet?

Yes. A spreadsheet can be an effective system of record for a stable, low-risk process. We can improve that workflow first and consider an integration or small application if volume, permissions or handoffs require it.

Is AI required?

No. Clear rules, ordinary automation and better ownership often solve the problem. AI is considered only where interpreting language or unstructured data adds value and the output can be checked.

How do we know the work helped?

We agree on a useful baseline before implementing changes, such as handling time, repeated entry, response delays, completion quality or exception volume, and compare what happens after the pilot.
