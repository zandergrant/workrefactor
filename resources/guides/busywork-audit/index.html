Free resource / Interactive audit

Find the busywork in your business

Identify the repeat entry, handoffs, waiting and unclear ownership that consume your team’s time. Leave with one experiment to run.

A process can look perfectly reasonable on paper and still eat hours every week. A request arrives, someone copies a few details, another person asks who owns it, and the team checks the status in three different places. No single step seems especially wasteful. Together, they turn straightforward work into a small coordination project.

This audit is a way to spot that friction without committing to a new platform or a six-week process-mapping exercise. Start with one recurring task, look at three recent examples, and work out what makes it harder than it needs to be.

1. Trace three real examples

Pick three instances of the same workflow, ideally one ordinary case, one that went smoothly and one that caused trouble. Walk through what actually happened rather than the process described in a procedure. For each case, capture:

Look for Ask yourself
The trigger Where did this work enter the business? Form, email, Slack, conversation?
The first owner Who knew they were responsible for picking it up?
Re-entry How many times did someone copy, reformat or look up the same information?
Waiting What happened while nobody was actively working on it?
Decisions Who could approve, reject or escalate the next step?
Exceptions What happened when the case didn't follow the normal path?
Finish line How did anyone know it was complete?

Use timestamps where they exist. Don't estimate twenty minutes of work simply because twenty minutes elapsed between two messages. Active effort and elapsed time are different measurements.

2. Identify the constraint before choosing the tool

There are five common patterns. They call for different fixes.

Pattern What it looks like Try first
Repeated entry Names, dates or status are copied between systems Use one source of truth; connect or import only when necessary
Missing ownership Requests linger because nobody is clearly responsible Add an owner and an agreed escalation rule
Invisible status People ask for updates because they cannot see progress Give them a shared, accurate view
Inconsistent decisions Similar cases get different treatment Write down the rule, examples and exception owner
Unnecessary step A report, approval or check exists because it always has Remove it and monitor what happens

A process that nobody needs should be retired, not automated. A process with unclear decision rights should be clarified before a bot starts forwarding reminders.

3. Run a small experiment

Write one sentence describing the problem: “We spend about 90 minutes each week finding and reconciling open facility requests before the Monday meeting.” Then make one change: a single intake form, a consistent ID, a summary view, or an explicit rule for who can close a request.

For two weeks, compare the same measures with your original three examples. Did the number of status chases fall? Did processing time drop? Did errors rise? Did the new approach move work onto somebody else? If the intervention helps, keep it. If it introduces new friction, revise or stop it.

4. Decide whether automation makes sense

Good early candidates are frequent, repeatable and rule-based, with information already available in a reasonably consistent format. Work that calls for nuanced judgment, handles sensitive data, or routinely produces new exceptions deserves more caution and human review.

Use the audit's annual-hours figure as a size-of-problem estimate, not promised savings. The figure assumes 48 operating weeks; it does not include waiting time, rework, interruption costs or the full labor burden. A useful pilot measures accepted output and real effort after the change.
