Write an SOP your team will use

This one-page format helps you document recurring work in a way the next person can actually follow, without creating a long manual.

An SOP often becomes less useful when it is written for the sake of documentation rather than for the person doing the job. Pages of background can obscure the few decisions that matter, and the instructions grow outdated when nobody owns them.

A good SOP makes a recurring task easier to perform correctly. It says when the work starts, who owns it, what a finished result looks like and what to do when something unexpected happens. You should be able to read the important part while the work is in front of you.

The one-page structure

Field The question it answers
Task and purpose What are we completing, and for whom?
Trigger What event tells us to start?
Owner Who is accountable for completing the task?
Required inputs What must be available before work begins?
Steps What actions happen, in order?
Definition of done What proves the result is correct?
Exceptions When should someone stop and ask for a decision?
Last reviewed Who checked this against a real case, and when?

Write it during real work

Observe someone completing a normal case. Capture only the steps that change the result or prevent a mistake. Then have another person follow the draft on a separate case without coaching from the author. Their questions are the most useful edits you can get.

If the SOP says “verify the account,” specify what verification means, where the source of truth lives and what to do when records conflict. If it says “notify the team,” name the channel and the minimum information required.

A short example

Task: Accept a new client handoff into delivery. Trigger: Sales marks the agreement signed. Required inputs: Contract link, service scope, primary contact, start date, committed deliverables and outstanding promises. Steps: Check completeness, assign a delivery owner, confirm readiness, send acknowledgement and record the next milestone. Definition of done: An assigned owner and agreed first milestone are visible in the shared delivery tracker. Exception: Missing scope or conflicting dates go back to the handoff owner before work begins.

Keep the SOP alive

Keep the SOP close to the work, perhaps linked from the request tracker, form or team workspace, and give someone responsibility for updating it when the process changes. A light review after a recurring exception can be more useful than an annual documentation exercise, especially when people need the instructions at the moment they are doing the task.

If the work can be simplified from ten steps to four, make that improvement before documenting the process so the SOP describes how the team should actually operate.

When the process is difficult to document

If the handoffs keep changing or nobody can agree on the actual steps, we can help trace how the work happens and clarify the rules before documenting or automating the process.
