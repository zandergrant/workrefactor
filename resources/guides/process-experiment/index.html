Your experiment brief

Preview the full export




The idea is simple: follow a few recent cases, change one thing, then compare the next set of cases. Start small enough that you can see what actually changed, without buying another tool or asking everyone to adopt a new process.


Start with a specific observation


“Our process is inefficient” is too broad to test. Instead, describe the recurring situation, the people affected and the point where effort or waiting accumulates. For example, a weekly report might take ninety minutes to prepare because its owner has to reconcile three different status sources. That gives you a place to investigate, rather than an instruction to automate reporting.


Try to distinguish three measures that are often mixed together: active effort, elapsed time and quality. A case might require five minutes of actual work but spend two days waiting for approval. Cutting the active work in half will not fix the waiting, and making it faster will not necessarily make it more accurate.


Change one thing you can observe


Choose the smallest intervention that plausibly addresses the cause. This could be one intake field, a single owner, a new escalation rule or the removal of a redundant check. You should be able to describe what is different in one sentence. If the test involves new software, restrict it to one part of the workflow so you can distinguish the tool from the process decisions around it.

Set a baseline before you make the change


Use a handful of recent examples that resemble the work you expect to see during the test. If possible, capture both an ordinary case and an awkward one. Record where the number came from, what counts as a case and when measurement starts and stops. Otherwise a change in how people count work can look like a change in performance.


For low-volume workflows, a few cases may be all you have. That's enough to learn about failure modes, but not enough to claim a statistically reliable improvement. If the cases are unusually easy, staffing changes or the team's workload shifts during the test, document those differences before making a decision.


Review the result, including what got worse


A target can help a team make a decision, but reaching it does not prove that your change caused the improvement. Look at the actual cases, check any guardrail you agreed in advance, and ask whether you shifted work onto somebody else. A faster handoff that creates errors for the receiving team is not a successful fix.


If the new approach looks promising, keep it small long enough to check whether the result holds. If not, look for the step that behaved differently than expected. Either outcome is useful when the next iteration is based on evidence rather than opinions.


Make the decision explicit


At the review, choose to keep, revise, extend or stop the change. Record the reason and the owner of any follow-up. If you keep it, update the documentation that people use during the work, not a separate presentation about the improvement.
