Free resource / Handoff agreement builder

Make a handoff work without another round of chasing.

Use a short diagnostic and build a one-page agreement between the team sending work and the team expected to pick it up.

A handoff can look complete to one team and still leave the next team unable to start. The record has been moved, a message has been sent, and everybody assumes somebody else has what they need. Then come the follow-ups, rework and reminders. Before adding automation, I'd want both sides to agree on what makes the work ready to receive.

Use this tool for one recurring handoff, such as sales to onboarding, support to engineering, or an internal request that moves between departments. Start with a case that recently went wrong, then use the agreement to make the next one easier.

Bring this to both teams, revise the wording together and test against several real cases before treating it as a standard.




Watch the handoff, not just the people handing it over


Pick a recent case where a transition stalled. Follow the case from the moment the first team considered it done to the moment the next team could do useful work. Notice which information was missing, who had to interpret the request, where updates were recorded and what happened when the case did not fit the normal pattern.


It is common for each team to have a sensible local process that doesn't quite connect to the other one. Sales may consider the deal ready when the agreement is signed; delivery may need dependencies, staffing and a confirmed start date. The fix isn't necessarily more diligence from either team, but a shared definition of readiness.


Define the receiving team's starting conditions


Agree on the smallest set of information the receiver needs to begin. Separate required inputs from nice-to-have context, and avoid making the sender fill out a long form nobody reads. A good acceptance rule should be easy to check from the actual record.


QuestionWorking answer


When does the handoff begin?A specific event changes the work from ready for preparation to ready for transfer.


What must travel with the case?The information needed for the receiver's first decision, including known constraints and links to the authoritative record.


Who owns it while waiting?One named role is accountable until the receiving role acknowledges acceptance.


How do we know it was accepted?A visible status change or action in the system the team actually uses.


What happens to incomplete cases?A defined return or escalation path that preserves the history of the original case.




Don't confuse sending with acceptance


An email or notification proves a message was sent, not that the receiving team has everything it needs. For important work, define a status such as Ready for review, Accepted or Returned for information. Keep those statuses in the authoritative record, rather than letting separate chat threads become the real source of truth.


Not every handoff needs a formal approval. A routine, low-risk transfer may be fine with a shared queue and clear assignment. Use explicit acceptance when missing details or unclear ownership regularly cause rework; don't add an approval step that creates a new bottleneck.


Test the agreement on five actual cases


Compare a few cases before and after the change, including an unusual case. Track how often work is returned for missing information, how long it waits before someone accepts it and how many clarifying messages are needed. For many teams, the useful result isn't an impressive score. It's that the person receiving the work no longer has to reconstruct what happened.


Keep an eye on the other side of the handoff. If the new checklist saves five minutes for the receiver but adds half an hour of data entry for the sender, you may have moved the burden rather than removed it. Aim for a short, reliable agreement supported by the systems already in place.


Automate only the dependable part


Once the trigger, required inputs, owner and exception path are clear, a simple automation may help route a record, alert the next person or flag a missing field. But the receiver must still be able to send a case back and the team needs a way to recover when systems disagree. A quiet failure is worse than a visible manual step.
