Internal tools.

Decide whether to keep the spreadsheet, connect existing systems, or build a small app, including the maintenance cost of each option.

Read the guide Choose the right approach ↓

Quick answer

What is an internal tool?

An internal tool is software built or configured to help employees carry out recurring work, whether that means managing requests, coordinating approvals, viewing information from several systems, or handling an activity that a general-purpose application doesn't support particularly well. In many businesses, the need appears gradually, as people begin copying data between tools or maintaining elaborate spreadsheets because no single place reflects how the work actually happens.

That doesn't automatically mean the business needs a new application. A shared spreadsheet can be perfectly suitable for a small team, and an existing CRM or project system may already offer the fields, permissions, and automation needed, provided someone takes the time to set it up around the process.

I would begin by watching how employees complete an actual task and identifying which steps create unnecessary effort, then choose the least complicated change that makes the work dependable. Sometimes the answer is a connection between existing systems, sometimes a more deliberate way of organizing information, and occasionally a small custom interface is worth the responsibility of maintaining it.

01 · The spectrum

Internal tools are not all custom software.

A useful internal tool can be as simple as a spreadsheet or as specialized as a custom application.

The mistake is treating “build software” as the default answer to an operational problem.

A spreadsheet may be ideal for a low-volume process owned by two people. A database or project platform might solve the next level of complexity. An integration may remove duplicate entry while letting each existing application keep doing its job.

Configuration can often go further than teams expect. Forms, views, rules, permissions, automations, dashboards, and native workflows can turn an existing platform into a useful operational system without creating a new codebase.

Custom software is worth it when the process matters, is specific to how you work, and is awkward enough in existing products that a focused interface clearly helps.

Four common levels

Escalate only when the process earns the complexity.

Level 1

Spreadsheet or shared table

Best for simple, visible, low-risk work with few users, modest volume, and limited permission or integration needs.

Level 2

Configured existing software

Use forms, fields, views, workflow rules, templates, dashboards, and permissions inside software the business already owns.

Level 3

Connected systems

Use automation, APIs, or integrations when the problem is information moving poorly between otherwise useful applications.

Level 4

Focused internal application

Build when employees need a purpose-built interface, logic, workflow, data model, or control layer that existing products cannot provide cleanly.

02 · Keep it simple

When a spreadsheet is enough.

I have a healthy respect for a good spreadsheet, particularly when a process is still changing and the people doing the work need to adjust it without commissioning software development. A shared table is familiar, inexpensive to maintain, and often more flexible than an application designed around assumptions that haven't yet been tested.

A small team tracking a manageable number of partner relationships may have little reason to move elsewhere, just as a finance team might reasonably prefer a spreadsheet for internal planning. The important questions are whether people can understand the information, whether ownership is clear, whether errors can be caught, and whether the volume and access requirements remain manageable.

Over time, though, a spreadsheet can quietly become responsible for far more than keeping records, with tabs representing separate departments, colors standing in for workflow rules, and employees copying information into other tools to make the next step happen. That is when I would examine what has become difficult, although the first improvement might still be a clearer structure or a simple integration rather than an entirely new application.

Spreadsheet test

Stay simple while these conditions remain true.

Users

Few people operate it

A small number of knowledgeable users can coordinate safely without complex access controls or role-based interfaces.

Volume

Workload is manageable

Rows, updates, calculations, and manual checks remain practical at the current volume.

Risk

Errors are recoverable

A deleted cell, wrong formula, or missed update is inconvenient rather than operationally dangerous.

Workflow

Coordination is simple

The process does not depend on complicated approvals, status transitions, queues, reminders, or handoffs.

Data

The structure is straightforward

The information fits naturally into a table and does not require many linked records, relationships, or conflicting versions.

Change

The process is still evolving

Keeping the system flexible may be more valuable than formalizing requirements too early.

Warning signs

Signs the spreadsheet has become the application.

Access

Different users need different permissions

Employees should not need access to an entire file just to update the small part of the process they own.

Data

Several tabs imitate a database

Complex lookups, duplicated identifiers, fragile formulas, and manually maintained relationships often indicate the data model has outgrown the sheet.

Workflow

Status depends on cell formatting

Color codes, comments, ad hoc columns, and manual reminders are often attempts to recreate workflow features the tool does not actually have.

Audit

Nobody can explain what changed

Important processes may need clear history around who changed a value, who approved an action, and when the status moved.

Integration

Employees repeatedly copy data elsewhere

If the spreadsheet sits between several systems, integration or a better source of truth may remove more work than another tab.

Scale

One mistake affects too much work

As volume and importance increase, fragile formulas, accidental edits, and unclear ownership become increasingly expensive.

03 · Integration

When systems should be connected.

Sometimes the business already has capable software, but employees are spending their time making the systems talk to each other. Sales may manage customers well in the CRM, finance may be comfortable with its accounting platform, and delivery may have a good project tool, yet closing a deal still requires somebody to recreate the same information in all three places and tell the next team that the work can begin.

I would follow a few recent handoffs to understand which records are copied, where people have to reconcile conflicting statuses, and what needs to happen when the original record changes. That usually makes the integration requirements much clearer, including which system should remain responsible for each piece of information and how the team will know when a connection fails.

Native integrations, APIs, lightweight scripts, or a small automation layer may be enough to move the necessary information while leaving people inside the tools they already know. There is little value in replacing three functioning systems merely because they need a better handoff, especially when a focused connection can remove the repeated effort with less cost and ongoing maintenance.

Integration signals

Connect systems when the friction is between them.

Duplicate entry

The same data is entered twice

Customer, employee, project, vendor, or transaction information should not require repeated manual recreation without a good reason.

Handoff

One system event starts work elsewhere

A signed contract, approved request, payment, status change, or completed task can often trigger the next system automatically.

Visibility

Employees reconcile competing statuses

If teams compare multiple systems just to determine what is true, the integration design or ownership of the record may be unclear.

Reporting

Data is manually assembled every week

Recurring exports, copy-and-paste reporting, and spreadsheet consolidation are common signals that systems should feed a shared reporting layer.

Coordination

Employees act as the API

When people’s recurring job is to notice a change in one tool and reproduce it somewhere else, the process may be ready for integration.

Source of truth

Each system has a legitimate role

Integration makes the most sense when existing tools are useful individually and the problem is how information moves between them.

04 · Build

When a focused internal application makes sense.

A custom internal application can be worthwhile when an important, recurring activity is hard to complete within the software a team already uses, and employees are compensating through so many workarounds that the process has become difficult to follow. In that situation, a focused interface can bring the relevant information and actions together, making the job easier without trying to replace every underlying system.

The strongest candidates tend to have a stable enough workflow to describe, clear ownership, and a measurable problem, whether that is time lost switching between tools, incomplete approvals, or recurring errors. A small application may combine data from several systems, apply specific business rules, or show different people only the decisions relevant to their roles, while the original platforms continue to hold the authoritative records.

I would be cautious about building something simply because a custom interface looks cleaner. Every new application creates obligations around permissions, uptime, data quality, changes, and user support, which means its benefit needs to justify those responsibilities over time. Starting with one complete task, testing it with the employees who actually perform it, and improving the design from what they experience is a more dependable route than attempting to build a comprehensive platform at once.

Build signals

Strong candidates share a few characteristics.

Importance

The process matters

It affects customers, revenue, cash, risk, capacity, employees, or another meaningful business outcome.

Frequency

Employees use it repeatedly

Small improvements become valuable when they apply to recurring work performed every day or week.

Fit

Existing products fit poorly

Available software requires too many workarounds, duplicate steps, unnecessary features, or unnatural process changes.

Complexity

The process spans data and roles

Employees need different views, permissions, workflow stages, calculations, records, or integrations that are difficult to coordinate.

Stability

The process is understood

The team can explain what the system should do, what good output looks like, and which exceptions matter.

Value

The improvement is measurable

Faster throughput, reduced labor, fewer errors, better control, or a stronger employee experience can justify the cost of ownership.

05 · Decision framework

Spreadsheet, integration, platform, or custom app?

When deciding between a spreadsheet, an integration, and a custom application, I would begin with the part of the work that is genuinely difficult. If the information is manageable and a small group can maintain it reliably, a spreadsheet may still be enough; if people are moving the same data between otherwise suitable tools, an integration is more likely to help.

When employees need a shared place to make decisions, track exceptions, or complete actions that cross several systems, there may be a case for a focused application, although I would still look carefully at what existing products can do with configuration. The price of a new tool includes the time spent understanding, securing, updating, and supporting it long after the first version works.

Quite often the right answer is a combination: a modest employee-facing interface on top of existing systems of record, connected by a few reliable automations. The choice should follow the workflow and the cost of maintaining the solution, with enough room to adjust as the team learns what it really needs.

Ask these first

Seven questions before building anything.

01

What operating problem are we solving?

Name the friction in business terms: duplicate entry, slow approvals, poor visibility, errors, scattered information, weak handoffs, or another real problem.

02

Can the process be simplified first?

Remove unnecessary steps, approvals, fields, duplicate records, and handoffs before deciding what software should support the future process.

03

Can our current tools already do this?

Check native capabilities, configuration, permissions, workflow rules, forms, views, and reporting before adding another system.

04

Is the problem really integration?

If existing tools work well individually, the solution may simply be moving data and status between them reliably.

05

Does the process need a new interface?

A custom application becomes more compelling when employees need one place to see context, perform actions, and manage the work.

06

Is the process stable enough to encode?

Do not harden a workflow into software while the business is still changing its rules every week.

07

Who will own the system after launch?

Every internal tool needs an owner for requirements, permissions, changes, bugs, adoption, data quality, and ongoing improvement.

06 · Examples

What useful internal tools look like in practice.

Operations

Request management

Employees submit structured requests, see status, provide missing information, and interact with the team responsible for resolution without relying on a shared inbox.

Client delivery

Client operations console

A focused interface combines account information, project status, open issues, key documents, billing state, and operational actions from several systems.

Finance

Approval workspace

Requests are validated, routed according to thresholds, displayed with relevant context, approved or rejected, and recorded with a clear audit trail.

Sales

Deal desk tool

Sales submits non-standard deal terms, pricing, legal requirements, and approvals through one structured process instead of coordinating separately across email and chat.

HR

Employee lifecycle tool

Managers and HR coordinate onboarding, role changes, equipment, access, offboarding, and required approvals while underlying systems remain connected.

Management

Operating review dashboard

Leaders see a curated set of operating measures, exceptions, trends, and underlying records instead of manually assembling recurring reports.

Knowledge work

Research workspace

Employees gather approved sources, structure findings, track review, generate standardized outputs, and preserve the final research record.

AI-assisted

Review queue

AI classifies, extracts, or drafts information in the background while employees review uncertain or consequential cases through a purpose-built interface.

Example

From five tools to one employee workflow.

An internal tool does not need to replace the systems underneath it.

Say an operations employee has to open the CRM, project system, billing platform, shared drive, and spreadsheet every time a client asks for a change.

Each underlying system has a legitimate purpose, the problem is what employees go through around the process.

A focused internal application can display the relevant customer, contract, project, billing, and request information in one place. The employee does the required action once, and APIs update the systems underneath.

The business avoids replacing its CRM or accounting software while still creating a workflow designed around how employees actually work.

That is often a better internal-tool strategy than rebuilding every system from scratch.

From tools I've built

The best tool may be the one nobody has to learn.

Some of the most useful internal tools I've built have been fairly small additions to software people already used. For global scheduling work, for example, I developed a spreadsheet-to-calendar synchronization workflow, a focused scheduling application, and a browser extension that helped turn messages into calendar events, with each one addressing a different source of recurring administrative effort rather than attempting to replace the entire system.

That experience fits with what I take from the Company of One philosophy: growing the operation or the software stack is not an achievement in itself, particularly if a capable team could do its work with fewer interruptions and a better arrangement of the tools it already has. Before sketching a new interface, I want to watch people perform the task and understand where they pause, repeat themselves, or leave the system to ask someone else what to do.

I would then build the smallest version that can complete the whole task, let people use it in normal work, and return to see what still requires correction or manual effort. Maintenance time matters here just as much as the apparent efficiency gain, because an impressive feature that needs constant attention can easily create more work than it removes, while a modest improvement that stays out of people's way can be valuable for years.

07 · Anatomy

What a good internal tool contains.

The interface is the part of an internal tool that employees see, but a dependable system also needs clear rules about where information comes from, who is allowed to change it, and what should happen when a case moves from one state to another. If the underlying records disagree or nobody knows which application owns a customer or project, a polished dashboard can make the confusion easier to see without actually resolving it.

I would give particular attention to the exceptions, because those are often what reveal whether a tool has been designed for real work. A missing document, a failed integration, an unusual approval, or an uncertain AI-generated result needs somewhere sensible to go, with a responsible person and enough history to understand how the case arrived there.

Someone also has to own the application after launch, including the decisions about permissions, changes, maintenance, and whether it is still worth using. I would rather see a small tool with those responsibilities understood and a dependable way to improve it than an elaborate interface that the team cannot confidently support.

Core components

Seven pieces matter more than the framework.

Interface

Focused employee experience

Show the information and actions relevant to the job instead of exposing every field and feature in the underlying systems.

Data

Clear sources of truth

Define which system owns customers, projects, transactions, users, documents, status, and other important records.

Workflow

Explicit states and handoffs

The application should reflect how work moves, who owns each state, and what conditions allow the process to advance.

Rules

Validation and business logic

Required information, calculations, approval rules, allowed values, and other deterministic logic should be enforced consistently.

Access

Permissions

Employees should see and change only the information appropriate to their role and the specific process.

Integration

Reliable system connections

APIs, webhooks, or workflow automation should move information deliberately between systems with clear handling for failures.

History

Audit and observability

Important actions, approvals, state changes, errors, and exceptions should leave enough history to understand what happened.

Build vs. buy

Existing software wins unless your process gives you a reason to build.

Custom software is not automatically better because it fits the process exactly on day one.

Commercial software spreads development, security, maintenance, support, reliability, and product improvement across many customers. That can make it much cheaper than owning the same capability yourself.

Buy or configure when the process is common and the available software handles it reasonably well.

Build when the workflow is specific enough to your operation that fitting the business into the product creates substantial ongoing friction, and that friction costs more than owning the software would.

The decision does not need to be all-or-nothing. A focused internal interface can sit on top of commercial systems and preserve their strengths while removing employee friction around the process.

Security and control

Internal does not mean low-risk.

Internal tools often sit closer to sensitive business data and operational actions than customer-facing software.

A tool may expose customer information, employee data, pricing, financial records, internal documents, or the ability to trigger consequential actions in other systems.

Define authentication, role-based access, least-needed permissions, audit history, data retention, backups, and what happens when an integration fails.

Avoid giving the application broad access simply because doing so is convenient during development. The tool should receive the minimum permissions needed to perform its job.

The more consequential the action, the more deliberate the approval, validation, and logging should be.

AI inside internal tools

AI is often more useful behind the interface than as the interface.

Employees should not always have to become expert prompters to benefit from AI.

A purpose-built internal tool can call AI at the point where interpretation is useful: classify a request, extract fields from a document, summarize an account, retrieve relevant internal knowledge, or prepare a first draft.

The rest of the application can remain structured. Employees see the relevant result, review uncertain cases, approve consequential outputs, and continue the process through normal controls.

This is often more reliable than sending employees to a blank chat box and expecting them to know the right prompt, source material, format, and validation steps every time.

ROI

How to determine whether an internal tool is worth building.

Measure the full cost of the current problem, time spent clicking is only part of it.

Estimate process frequency, employee time, manual coordination, errors, rework, delays, reporting effort, and the capacity constraints created by the current system.

Then estimate what the proposed tool changes. Does it reduce data entry? Shorten cycle time? Prevent missed steps? Allow one employee to manage more volume? Improve controls? Remove the need to reconcile several systems?

Subtract the full ownership cost: design, development, infrastructure, software services, maintenance, monitoring, support, security, and future changes.

If the process is low-volume and the spreadsheet works, the ROI of custom software may be negative. If 40 employees lose hours every week to the same broken workflow, the economics can look very different.

08 · Implementation

How to design and build an internal tool.

01

Start with the process.

Define the operating problem, the desired outcome, who performs the work, what systems are involved, and where the current process creates friction.

02

Simplify before building.

Remove unnecessary steps, duplicate entry, weak approvals, redundant fields, and avoidable handoffs before encoding the future process in software.

03

Decide what should remain existing software.

Identify the current systems of record and avoid rebuilding capabilities that commercial software already performs well.

04

Define the smallest useful interface.

Build around the employee’s actual job. Show only the context and actions needed to perform the process instead of recreating a giant enterprise platform.

05

Model data and permissions deliberately.

Define records, relationships, sources of truth, roles, allowed actions, and the minimum access the application requires.

06

Build the core workflow first.

Implement the normal path, integrations, validation, and state transitions before trying to support every edge case and optional feature.

07

Test with real users.

Watch employees perform the actual work. Look for missing context, unnecessary clicks, confusing states, permission problems, and cases where users return to old workarounds.

08

Measure and maintain.

Track adoption, cycle time, errors, throughput, exceptions, support needs, and system reliability. Assign an owner who can keep the application aligned with the business process.

Common mistakes

Where internal tools go wrong.

A new internal tool can become surprisingly complicated when development starts before the team has agreed how the work should happen. One stakeholder asks for a report, another wants an approval queue, and someone else wants to replace a spreadsheet, until a modest request has turned into a small enterprise platform that is expensive to maintain and still depends on the original workarounds.

I would be particularly cautious about rebuilding services that already work, such as customer management, accounting, identity, or document storage, because connecting those products is often a better choice than taking ownership of their functionality. The new tool also needs to be clear about which systems hold the correct records, who may see or change sensitive information, and what happens when an integration fails or an unusual request needs a person to intervene.

The best test is to watch employees use the tool for the actual task it was built to support, paying attention to the extra clicks, private notes, and side spreadsheets that remain. A technically impressive design has limited value if people still need to work around it, and the responsibility continues long after launch through support, permissions, monitoring, and changes to the underlying process. I would rather improve a small tool through repeated observation than build a larger one whose complexity nobody has time to own.

FAQ

Internal tools questions.

01

What is an internal tool?

An internal tool is software built or configured for employees to perform recurring business work more reliably, often by combining workflow, data, rules, permissions, and a focused interface.

02

When is a spreadsheet enough?

A spreadsheet is often enough when the process is low-volume, owned by a small number of people, low-risk, easy to understand, and does not require complicated permissions, workflow, or integrations.

03

When should systems be integrated?

Connect systems when the primary problem is duplicate entry, recurring data movement, broken handoffs, or manual reconciliation between tools that otherwise perform their individual jobs well.

04

When should you build an internal application?

Build when the process is important, recurring, reasonably stable, poorly served by available software, and valuable enough to justify ongoing ownership and maintenance.

05

Should you build or buy?

Use existing software when it solves the problem well enough, and configure and connect tools before building. Build when your operating process is specific enough that general products create substantial ongoing friction.

06

Can an internal tool use existing systems underneath?

Yes. Often the best design is a focused employee interface that connects to existing systems of record through APIs or automations instead of replacing them.

07

Can internal tools include AI?

Yes. AI can handle bounded tasks such as classification, extraction, summarization, drafting, or knowledge retrieval while the application manages permissions, workflow, validation, and human review.

08

How do you know if an internal tool is worth it?

Compare the full cost of the current process with the expected improvement in labor, speed, quality, capacity, control, and employee experience, then subtract the long-term cost of owning the software.
