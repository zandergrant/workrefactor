How to Fix Team Handoffs That Keep Losing Information

Sales finishes its part of a job and hands it to delivery, but the receiving team still has to ask what was promised, where the files live, or who owns the next decision. The work has moved between teams without becoming ready for the next step.

The situation

Illustrative business scenario

A fictional services firm signs a new client. Sales marks the deal as won, sends a celebratory email, and assumes delivery will take over. The delivery lead can see the client name and start date, but not the scope exceptions or the promises made during negotiation. The first internal kickoff becomes an exercise in reconstructing the agreement.

Adding an onboarding checklist can help, provided the checklist is tested against what the receiving team truly needs. The important redesign is an acceptance point: delivery should be able to say the handoff is ready or return it for specified missing information before the work is silently passed along.

What changes in the workflow?

Completion for the sending team is not the same as readiness for the receiving team. A clear acceptance step aligns those definitions and prevents missing information from surfacing later as emergency coordination.

How to recognize this problem

Before changing a tool or policy, look for these patterns in recent work. They help distinguish the actual constraint from the symptom everybody has learned to work around.

  • The receiving team regularly schedules meetings to find missing context.
  • A customer repeats information already shared during the sales process.
  • Work is marked done upstream but remains blocked downstream.

Where I'd look first

Follow three handoffs that went well and three that did not. Ask the recipient to identify what they could not do immediately and which information would have prevented the delay. Resist copying every available field into the handoff; focus on what changes the next action.

A practical way to redesign it

  1. Define ready

    Write the minimum conditions that make the next team able to start, including records, decisions, accountable owner and agreed first milestone.

  2. Capture commitments

    List exceptions and customer promises in a structured field with a link to the authoritative agreement.

  3. Introduce acceptance

    Allow the receiving owner to accept or return the handoff with a reason, so incomplete work does not quietly accumulate.

  4. Close the feedback loop

    Review returns with the sending team and remove fields that nobody uses while tightening information that routinely goes missing.

How to tell if the change helped

Choose one or two measures before starting, gather a baseline from the current workflow, and check what happened after the change. The most useful evidence is accepted work with fewer problems, not the number of automated actions or new dashboard charts.

Tradeoffs and failure cases

Where this approach can go wrong

A form with thirty mandatory questions can turn a manageable process into a new administrative burden. Keep the required fields narrow, protect sensitive contract details, and make sure the acceptance step is a real decision rather than a checkbox everyone clicks automatically.

A smaller alternative

Where work changes substantially between customers, distinguish a small common core from a case-specific exception note. Forcing every unusual engagement into the same detailed template can hide the nuance that matters most.

One experiment worth running

For one fortnight, use a five-field handoff brief and require explicit acceptance by the receiving owner. Record which missing items still trigger follow-up and revise the brief based on those cases. The first draft should be treated as an experiment, not a final policy.
