How to Stop Chasing Project Status Updates at Work

A manager asks for an update because the tracker is unreliable, and the employee doesn't update the tracker because the manager keeps asking directly. Before long, the team is maintaining two versions of the same status, one in the work and another in messages.

What this looks like at work

A familiar example

An operations team is coordinating dozens of client requests. Each person tracks their own work in a different place, while the team lead builds a summary spreadsheet every week. By Thursday the summary is already out of date, and the manager is sending messages to check which requests are blocked.

I wouldn't build a dashboard yet. First I'd ask what the team means by 'in progress' or 'blocked', who changes those statuses and when. Without that agreement, a more attractive dashboard will simply display the same unreliable information.

What changes in the workflow?

I'd make the status update part of a meaningful handoff, such as accepting a request, recording a decision or marking something blocked. Give each case an owner and next action, and the team can review exceptions without recreating the whole picture every week.

Signs this is happening on your team

  • A weekly report is recreated from information already stored in other places.
  • Meetings are dominated by asking whether something is done.
  • Employees receive status questions about records they updated earlier.

Where I'd start

Start with the three questions managers ask most often, then find out where each answer comes from and why it's hard to trust. Sometimes the information exists and is difficult to locate; other times nobody records it in the first place. Those require different fixes.

What I'd try first

  1. Define a minimum record

    Use one owner, a small set of unambiguous statuses, a next action and a date. Avoid a field unless someone will act on it.

  2. Update at the source

    Make updating the shared status part of the work transition, such as accepting a request or recording a decision.

  3. Design for exceptions

    Highlight blocked, overdue and ownerless records rather than making someone read every healthy case.

  4. Retire a report

    Once the shared view is trusted, deliberately stop preparing one redundant summary. Otherwise the new system adds work instead of replacing it.

How to tell whether it helped

Status requestsCount one-to-one messages asking for information that should be visible already.
FreshnessShare of active cases updated within an agreed interval or on meaningful state changes.
Reporting effortTime spent assembling weekly summaries, including corrections.

What to watch out for

One thing to watch

Too much visibility can become surveillance. The goal is to make coordination easier, not record every minute an employee spends on a task. Agree on meaningful state changes and give teams room to perform the work without constant updates.

When a simpler fix is enough

If employees already manage their work in a reliable system, I'd start with a read-only view rather than asking them to update another tracker. A shared sheet can be useful, but it shouldn't become one more competing version of the truth.

One change to test this week

Choose one team and one type of request, agree on four statuses, and make an owner and next action required for open work. Replace one routine status meeting with a review of blocked and overdue requests, then see whether people actually spend less time chasing updates.
