How to Fix Approval Bottlenecks Without More Reminders

A purchase, customer exception or project change sits waiting for approval, even though nobody is actively reviewing it. The usual response is another reminder. The deeper problem is often that the decision has no clear owner or is being sent to more people than necessary.

The situation

Illustrative business scenario

In an illustrative professional-services firm, a project manager needs approval for a modest change in scope. The request passes through a team lead, a department head and finance, although only the department head can decide whether the change is acceptable. Finance receives the request without the cost information it needs, sends it back, and the project manager restarts the email chain.

A new workflow tool could move those emails into a queue. It would not, by itself, fix the extra approval or the missing information. The meaningful redesign is to understand which decisions require review, who is authorized to make them, and what information must be present when the request reaches that person.

What changes in the workflow?

The redesigned flow moves decision rights into a documented rule and ensures the approver receives the material needed to act. A status is useful, but only after the team has decided what that status means and who can change it.

How to recognize this problem

Before changing a tool or policy, look for these patterns in recent work. They help distinguish the actual constraint from the symptom everybody has learned to work around.

  • Requests are regularly forwarded to someone else without a decision.
  • Approvers ask for the same missing details on most submissions.
  • People escalate by seniority rather than by risk or authority.

Where I'd look first

Choose ten recent requests and mark who actually made the decision, who merely forwarded the request, how many times clarification was requested and how long the case waited between responses. Separate review work from queue time.

A practical way to redesign it

  1. Classify decisions

    Distinguish routine, reversible changes from decisions with meaningful financial, legal or customer consequences.

  2. Document authority

    Write a short decision matrix with thresholds, substitutes when the owner is unavailable, and circumstances requiring escalation.

  3. Require the right context

    Ask actual approvers which information they need; make those fields part of intake rather than requesting them after the handoff.

  4. Set a visible exception route

    A request that exceeds a threshold or has conflicting information should enter an explicit review path, not disappear into an inbox.

How to tell if the change helped

Choose one or two measures before starting, gather a baseline from the current workflow, and check what happened after the change. The most useful evidence is accepted work with fewer problems, not the number of automated actions or new dashboard charts.

Waiting timeTrack time between complete submission and first decision, excluding time spent collecting missing inputs.
Unnecessary touchesCount forwarding and sign-off steps without a distinct decision.
ReworkRecord returns for clarification and decisions reversed because context was missing.

Tradeoffs and failure cases

Where this approach can go wrong

Not every approval is waste. Controls around financial exposure, privacy, safety or regulated actions may be mandatory, and replacing them with a simple threshold could create risk. Clarify those requirements with the responsible policy or compliance owner before changing the route.

A smaller alternative

For a small team, an agreed decision table and a shared status column can be sufficient. Automation becomes valuable when requests are frequent enough for manual routing to create real work, and when exceptions are clearly defined.

One experiment worth running

Run the next ten routine approvals through a single-page decision brief that requires purpose, cost, owner and requested date. Compare waiting time and clarification loops with the previous ten cases. Only then decide whether automated routing would help.
