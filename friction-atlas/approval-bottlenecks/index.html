How to Fix Approval Bottlenecks Without More Reminders

A small purchase or project change sits waiting for approval, so someone sends another reminder. The request may be perfectly reasonable; the problem is that nobody is sure who can make the decision or what information they need.

What this looks like at work

A familiar example

Picture a project manager asking to approve a modest change in scope. The request goes to a team lead, then a department head, then finance, although only the department head can authorize the change. Finance needs a cost estimate that wasn't included, so it goes back to the project manager and the email chain starts again.

Putting those emails into an approval tool would make the queue easier to see, but the same unnecessary steps would still be there. I'd first look at which decisions genuinely need review, who has the authority to make them, and what the approver needs to know before the request reaches them.

What changes in the workflow?

In the revised process, the person making the request supplies the important context upfront, and the request goes directly to the right decision owner. The team has also agreed when a case needs escalation, which makes the status meaningful rather than just another label.

Signs this is happening on your team

  • Requests are regularly forwarded to someone else without a decision.
  • Approvers ask for the same missing details on most submissions.
  • People escalate by seniority rather than by risk or authority.

Where I'd start

I'd take the last ten approvals and write down who made the decision, who passed the request along, how often it came back for clarification, and how long it sat untouched. That separates the time spent making a decision from time spent waiting for someone to pick it up.

What I'd try first

  1. Classify decisions

    Distinguish routine, reversible changes from decisions with meaningful financial, legal or customer consequences.

  2. Document authority

    Write a short decision matrix with thresholds, substitutes when the owner is unavailable, and circumstances requiring escalation.

  3. Require the right context

    Ask actual approvers which information they need; make those fields part of intake rather than requesting them after the handoff.

  4. Set a visible exception route

    A request that exceeds a threshold or has conflicting information should enter an explicit review path, not disappear into an inbox.

How to tell whether it helped

Waiting timeTrack time between complete submission and first decision, excluding time spent collecting missing inputs.
Unnecessary touchesCount forwarding and sign-off steps without a distinct decision.
ReworkRecord returns for clarification and decisions reversed because context was missing.

What to watch out for

One thing to watch

Not every approval is waste. Controls around financial exposure, privacy, safety or regulated actions may be mandatory, and replacing them with a simple threshold could create risk. Clarify those requirements with the responsible policy or compliance owner before changing the route.

When a simpler fix is enough

For a smaller team, a decision table and a shared status column may do the job. I'd only automate the routing once the requests are frequent enough, and the exceptions clear enough, that people are spending meaningful time sending them around.

One change to test this week

For the next ten routine approvals, use a short brief with the purpose, estimated cost, decision owner and requested date. Compare them with the previous ten cases, especially the waiting time and clarification loops; if the new process works, consider whether routing it automatically would save more effort.
