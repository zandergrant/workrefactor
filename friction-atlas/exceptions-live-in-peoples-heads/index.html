How to Fix Processes That Depend on One Person Knowing Every Exception

The procedure is clear until something unusual happens, and then everyone looks for the colleague who knows how the last case was handled. Over time that person becomes the unofficial exception process, even though the rest of the team is perfectly capable of doing the ordinary work.

The situation

Illustrative business scenario

Consider a client operations team with a standard process for schedule changes. Most requests fit the rules, but some customers have special contractual terms. The same experienced coordinator handles those requests every time, partly because the rules were never documented and partly because the context is scattered across old messages.

We could add more detail to the main procedure, but that might make everyday cases slower without helping the unusual ones. I'd keep the normal path simple and explain when a case needs a different decision, along with who has the authority and what context they need.

What changes in the workflow?

I'd preserve room for judgment while making recurring exceptions easier to recognize. That could mean a short decision guide, a named escalation owner and a record of why similar cases were handled differently, without building a rigid rule for every possible situation.

How to recognize this problem

  • Work slows whenever one particular colleague is unavailable.
  • New employees can follow the SOP but do not know when it is safe to deviate.
  • Similar exceptions receive different decisions without any documented reason.

Where I'd look first

I'd look at the last ten unusual cases and ask what made them different, who decided, what information mattered and whether the same situation has come up before. Group the cases that are genuinely similar; there's little value in writing a new rule for every one-off problem.

What I'd change first

  1. Define the ordinary path

    Keep the main SOP simple and show the conditions under which it does not apply.

  2. Identify decision authority

    For each exception category, name the person or role able to approve a deviation and any non-negotiable boundaries.

  3. Record decisions briefly

    Capture the reason, applicable policy and relevant evidence without transcribing every conversation.

  4. Review recurring patterns

    If the same exception appears often, decide whether the standard workflow should change or whether a clearer playbook entry is enough.

How I'd measure the difference

Expert interruptionsHow often colleagues must stop the designated expert for a recurring exception.
Decision consistencyDifferences in outcomes for similar cases, with appropriate context.
Time to resolutionElapsed time from exception identification to an accountable decision.

Tradeoffs and failure cases

What I'd be careful about

Some exceptions should remain case-by-case, especially where contractual, privacy or safety obligations require judgment. Do not publish sensitive customer examples as training material without authorization. An exhaustive decision tree can become harder to maintain than a short escalation protocol.

When something simpler is enough

For rare, high-stakes exceptions, a clear escalation route may be enough. If similar cases happen all the time, a short, searchable set of approved examples can help the rest of the team handle them without interrupting the same person.

One experiment worth running

Pick the three exceptions that generate the most questions and write a one-page guide for each, with the decision owner and boundaries made clear. Use the guides for two weeks, recording where people still need help or where a reviewer has to step in.
