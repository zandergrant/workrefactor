How to Fix Processes That Depend on One Person Knowing Every Exception

The documented workflow looks straightforward until something unusual happens. Then everybody turns to the one colleague who remembers how a similar case was handled last year, what the customer was promised, and which rule can be bent.

Download team worksheet ↓Share on X ↗

The situation

Illustrative business scenario

A client-operations team has a standard procedure for granting schedule changes. Most requests follow it cleanly, but enterprise customers sometimes have special contractual arrangements. The same experienced coordinator handles these cases because the rules have never been written down and the relevant context sits in old messages.

The team could add more steps to the main procedure, but that may make ordinary cases slower while still failing to explain why exceptions are treated differently. A better system separates the predictable path from the decision points where an unusual case should be reviewed by someone with clear authority.

What changes in the workflow?

The improvement preserves judgment where it matters while making routine exceptions easier to recognize. The goal is not to turn every unusual situation into a rigid rule; it is to stop repeatedly rediscovering the same decision without retaining the reasoning.

How to recognize this problem

Before changing a tool or policy, look for these patterns in recent work. They help distinguish the actual constraint from the symptom everybody has learned to work around.

  • Work slows whenever one particular colleague is unavailable.
  • New employees can follow the SOP but do not know when it is safe to deviate.
  • Similar exceptions receive different decisions without any documented reason.

Where I'd look first

For the last ten unusual cases, write down what made each case different, who decided the outcome, what information they used and whether the case has occurred before. Group genuinely similar exceptions rather than writing a rule for every anecdote.

A practical way to redesign it

  1. Define the ordinary path

    Keep the main SOP simple and show the conditions under which it does not apply.

  2. Identify decision authority

    For each exception category, name the person or role able to approve a deviation and any non-negotiable boundaries.

  3. Record decisions briefly

    Capture the reason, applicable policy and relevant evidence without transcribing every conversation.

  4. Review recurring patterns

    If the same exception appears often, decide whether the standard workflow should change or whether a clearer playbook entry is enough.

How to tell if the change helped

Choose one or two measures before starting, gather a baseline from the current workflow, and check what happened after the change. The most useful evidence is accepted work with fewer problems, not the number of automated actions or new dashboard charts.

Expert interruptionsHow often colleagues must stop the designated expert for a recurring exception.
Decision consistencyDifferences in outcomes for similar cases, with appropriate context.
Time to resolutionElapsed time from exception identification to an accountable decision.

Tradeoffs and failure cases

Where this approach can go wrong

Some exceptions should remain case-by-case, especially where contractual, privacy or safety obligations require judgment. Do not publish sensitive customer examples as training material without authorization. An exhaustive decision tree can become harder to maintain than a short escalation protocol.

A smaller alternative

Where exceptions are rare and high-stakes, an explicit escalation rule may be more useful than a large knowledge base. Where they are common and repeatable, a searchable collection of short approved examples can help new employees act with confidence.

One experiment worth running

Choose the three exception types responsible for the most interruptions and create one-page decision briefs with an owner and clear boundaries. Use them on real cases for two weeks, recording where the brief was insufficient or where a reviewer still needed to intervene.
