The problem

Growth creates operational debt.

As teams grow, responsibilities shift and workarounds become routine. Here are some common signs that the way work gets done needs attention.

  • Too many spreadsheets
  • Information copied between tools by hand
  • Repetitive reporting
  • Approvals buried in inboxes
  • Processes that live in one person’s memory
  • Disconnected systems
  • Unclear ownership
  • Poor visibility into work
  • Workflows that break as volume grows
  • New software added without fixing the process

Selected work

Experience put to work.

These projects come from my experience leading operations, including launch readiness, global scheduling, support costs, and recurring customer issues.

Giving complex launches one clear view of readiness

Coordinating multiple enterprise launches meant tracking staffing, training, deadlines, and readiness across teams. The information existed, but it was spread across spreadsheets, reporting systems, and conversations. Getting a reliable picture of progress took unnecessary coordination.

I designed and built an internal operations hub that brought readiness data, capacity, upcoming milestones, and risk indicators into one shared view. It connected existing reporting and learning systems, while letting teams continue updating information through familiar workflows rather than requiring another parallel process.

What changed: Stakeholders could see readiness across concurrent launches, identify risks earlier, and coordinate decisions from a more consistent view of the work.

Building dependable scheduling infrastructure for global delivery

Running programs across regions and multiple launch waves required more than putting meetings on calendars. Teams needed accurate schedules, clear assignments, and confidence that changes were reflected wherever the information was used.

I designed a connected scheduling workflow that turned structured delivery plans into synchronized calendars. AI-assisted development helped me build tools for time-zone handling, changes to facilitator assignments, duplicate prevention, and validation. Scheduling decisions stayed with the people responsible for delivery; repeatable coordination and record maintenance moved into the system.

What changed: A more reliable scheduling operation that supported large volumes of sessions across multiple launch waves, with less manual upkeep and clearer visibility into delivery plans.

Improving the economics of customer support

In a high-volume support organization, reducing costs without compromising service meant understanding what was actually driving demand, effort, and spending. Total ticket volume and staffing levels alone couldn't explain where the most valuable improvements would come from.

As a support director, I developed an efficiency strategy and worked with Analytics on a performance framework connecting customer demand, self-service, productivity, resolution effort, and operating cost. That work informed changes to productivity expectations, resource allocation, automation, and reporting, with customer experience kept in view alongside efficiency.

What changed: The broader improvement effort delivered approximately $300K in realized operating savings. Customer satisfaction and first-response performance also exceeded benchmarks during the documented improvement period.

Reducing recurring work by fixing the process behind it

A recurring category of enterprise support requests was consuming significant attention. Handling each request faster would have helped, but it wouldn't have addressed the policy and process contributing to the demand.

I coordinated the support-side implementation of a cross-functional change, aligning exception handling, escalation decisions, team procedures, and customer guidance with the new approach. The work required operational coordination across several functions, not another layer of automation.

What changed: Within a few months, that issue was no longer the leading enterprise support ticket category in the tracked reporting period. The improvement came from reducing avoidable demand at its source.

How we work

Start with the people and the work, then the tools.

We start by tracing the work, locating the source of friction, and agreeing on what needs to change before choosing tools.

Understand the work

We follow the process with the people who use it, looking at where information gets lost, decisions stall, or someone has to step in to keep things moving.

Simplify the process

Together, we work out which steps are useful, what can be removed, and where ownership needs to be clearer, making the most of the systems you already have.

Test and improve

We put a manageable change into practice, look at what the data and the people doing the work tell us, and refine it before expanding it further.

The aim is to give your team more room for focused work, with fewer things to maintain. We build around how people actually work and keep improving the process as we learn what helps.
